# Kaizen coaching videos: research notes for LevelMeUp's workouts

*Compiled 2026-10-11 for M6c from the owner's 15 Kaizen lesson videos ("Coach Cho"), transcribed on the laptop with
Whisper (`D:\Kaizen Videos\transcripts`, kept out of the repo) and paraphrased by five research passes. Kaizen is a paid
program: these notes keep facts and principles (numbers, rules, exercise names) in our own words and never its wording,
stories, member names, brand lines or links. Do not ship any of it as Kaizen content. Timestamps (~[mm:ss]) point into
the transcripts; many lessons are in Taglish, so wording is translated. Companion to `kaizen-training.md` (the
spreadsheets and PDFs), whose §5 these notes extend and in places contradict.*

## Plain summary

- The training message is simple and consistent across the lessons: lift moderately heavy (about 6–15 reps), rest 2–3
  minutes between sets, keep the same exercises long enough to beat your last numbers, add a rep, a little weight or a
  set over time, keep good form, and train 3–4 times a week for months rather than 6 times a week for two weeks.
- Small progress counts: the same weight with better range, one more rep, one more pound, one more set. Judge progress
  by the trend over 3–4 weeks, not one session.
- Deload regularly: beginners every 5–8 weeks, by dropping a set or two and a few reps (the workout builder keeps the
  weights). Judge fatigue over a week, never a single day.
- On a bad day, do a minimum session (10 reps of push-ups or squats) rather than nothing.
- Walking is the preferred cardio; build steps up 1,000 at a time from where you are. HIIT and circuits are discouraged.
- Men and women train by the same principles; lifting doesn't make women bulky. The men's and women's builders differ
  only in which body parts get more slots.
- Several lessons also contain advice that a beginner app without a coach must not give: sets to failure on everything,
  short sleep called acceptable, very aggressive diets, supplement and medical claims, shaming and money penalties. The
  "risky" lists below collect them.

## What LevelMeUp takes into M6c, and what it leaves out

| Lesson point | In M6c |
|---|---|
| 6–15 reps at a challenging load, progressive overload | Each exercise has a rep range (mostly the workout builder's); the cue says to go a little heavier once every set reaches the top. |
| Small progress counts (+1 rep, better range) | The workout log starts each set at the bottom of the range; any honest number counts. |
| Rest 2–3 minutes between sets | The session's steps say rest about 2 minutes, up to 3 after the first, biggest exercise; the minutes estimate allows 2.5 minutes a set. |
| Keep the same exercises so progress can be tracked | The exercise for each slot is chosen by fixed rules, so it stays the same until the player's rung or equipment changes. |
| 3–4 sessions a week, consistency over intensity | A Warrior's day pattern gives about 3–4 workouts a week; other classes get fewer. Workouts rotate through the program's days (A, B, C). |
| Minimum session on low days | The hard-day version of a workout is one set of each exercise. |
| No failure for beginners (our rule; the lessons push failure) | Every cue says to stop with 1–2 good reps left. Nothing in the app says "to failure". |
| Men vs women: same principles, different emphasis | "Men's program", "Women's program" and "No preference" are layouts anyone can pick, each with a plain line saying what it emphasises. |
| Deload every 5–8 weeks for beginners | Not in M6c: rungs rise only on demonstrated readiness, and hard days, "Too hard" and step-downs ease off. A planned deload waits for real data from the pilot (roadmap). |
| Steps +1,000 at a time; walking over HIIT | Not in M6c (a steps quest is a later milestone). The walking ladders already prefer walking. |
| Weekly fatigue trend, daily logs | Not in M6c (an evening check-in is a later idea). |
| Rejected outright | Sets to failure, short sleep as acceptable, deficits of 500–1,000 kcal or very low intakes, supplements, medical and lab advice, belts and breath-holding, self-managed injuries, shaming tasks and money penalties, rewards for weight change. |

### 1. The Simplified Workout, Nutrition, Cardio, and Supplementation Program (Week 2 lesson, 1:43:16)

**Topic:** The minimum you need to know about lifting, calories and protein, cardio and steps, and supplements. The
whole lesson rests on one idea: consistency matters more than intensity.

#### Training rules
- Track fitness numbers you can measure every day or week (strength, weight, body fat, measurements) rather than vague
  "health" goals. Measurable progress gives quick wins, and quick wins keep people consistent (~[11:01]–[17:08]).
- Build up one level at a time instead of jumping into an extreme routine. Quick wins like losing 1–3 lb or adding
  5–20 lb to a lift build belief (~[12:46]–[14:20]).
- Lifting has one job: making muscles grow. That holds even when the goal is fat loss, because losing fat without
  muscle leaves a thin, saggy look (~[17:46]–[20:25]).
- There is no separate fat-loss, muscle-building or "toning" workout. The same lifts (deadlift, squat, overhead press,
  pull-ups, dips are named) serve every goal. Only diet and activity outside the gym decide whether weight goes up or
  down (~[21:45]–[23:38]).
- Women won't get bulky from lifting (he cites roughly 10× less testosterone than men). He tells women to lift instead
  of only doing cardio (~[19:41]–[21:30]).
- Muscle comes from following a proper program consistently. A perfect program you quit in week 3 is worth nothing
  (~[24:09]–[24:44]).
- Consistency over intensity: training 3× a week for 3 months beats 6× a week for 2 weeks. The same applies to diet
  (~[26:17]–[26:41]).
- Pick a program you can follow for 100 days without missing. Starting at 2 sessions a week is fine; move to 3–4 if 2
  feels too easy (~[28:09]–[28:31]).
- **Frequency ranking**, if time and recovery allow: 4 sessions a week beats 3, and 3 beats 2. Going from 4 to 5 adds
  very little, so 4 is their ideal. Use 2 a week only as a fallback for very busy weeks or travel, so the week isn't
  a zero (~[28:35]–[29:13], repeated ~[33:43]–[34:02]).
- Workout builder walkthrough (~[29:15]–[32:45]): new lifters should read the warm-up, starting-weight, logging and
  rules tabs first. Each exercise slot has a drop-down of substitutes, for example:
  - goblet squat → squat
  - barbell shoulder press → dumbbells or bands at home
  - chin-up → lat pulldown until chin-ups are possible
  - incline bench → push-up
- **Progression numbers** read out from the builder (~[31:35]–[32:29]):
  - Bodyweight moves (decline push-ups, full pull-ups): every set to failure or close to it. At 15–20 reps, add
    5–10 lb; at 10–15 reps, stay where you are. (He doesn't say how to add load to a bodyweight move, presumably a
    vest, a belt or a harder variant.)
  - Weighted example with an 8-rep target: 10+ reps on the last set means add 5–10 lb next week; fewer than 8 (say 7)
    means take off 5–10 lb next week.
  - Weeks 1–5 progress; week 6 is a deload (explained in the Week 6 lesson).
- Film your sets to check technique. The worst outcome is finding out on day 100 that your form was wrong all along
  (~[33:00]–[33:11]).
- If you already know another program's exercises, keep using them, since familiar exercises help. The builder mainly
  adds options for people training at home without a gym (~[33:12]–[33:43]).
- Rest-day options (~[34:21]–[34:58]):
  - go to the gym just to stretch or walk on a treadmill (keeps the gym habit going);
  - hit the step target;
  - play a sport or do cardio you enjoy, keeping in mind that hard activity eats into recovery;
  - do any abs or calf work you skipped.
- Little or no value (~[35:04]–[36:03]): daily ab workouts (you can't burn fat from one spot), fasted cardio (no extra
  fat loss), drop sets, forced reps, 10-second rests and similar techniques, a "magic" rep count, sweating or sauna
  suits, and a "best" time of day to train.

#### Cardio, steps, sleep and recovery
- Cardio has two purposes: heart and overall health (mood included) and burning calories (~[1:22:46]–[1:23:27]).
- Cardio doesn't burn fat by itself; the calorie deficit does. His example: you need 1,800 kcal to lose weight but
  usually eat 2,200, so 30 min of cardio burning 400 kcal brings you to 1,800 (~[1:23:45]–[1:25:00]). If diet alone
  creates the deficit, cardio is optional (~[1:25:03]–[1:25:44]).
- Hours of cardio can't make up for overeating (eat 4,000 kcal, burn 1,500, and you're still far over target), and
  the fatigue hurts lifting, energy and motivation (~[1:25:50]–[1:27:11]).
- When cardio makes sense (~[1:27:21]–[1:28:38]):
  - you enjoy it (sports);
  - you want to eat more;
  - you ate too much on one day and want to offset it;
  - weight loss has stalled and you'd rather move more than eat less.
- Cardio is not required. Too much of it hurts recovery directly (less energy left to lift) and indirectly (it adds
  to the stress you already carry from dieting, lifting, work and family) (~[1:28:40]–[1:29:37]).
- Not recommended: HIIT and circuit training. Both are very tiring and burn no more fat than low-intensity cardio
  (~[1:29:40]–[1:29:56]).
- The best cardio is walking. 10,000 steps a day burns about 200–500 kcal with little cost to recovery
  (~[1:30:01]–[1:30:37]).
- Practical step tips:
  - a treadmill or walking pad at home, so you can walk while watching shows or working (~[1:31:01]–[1:31:29]);
  - make steps part of the daily routine;
  - split them into blocks, for example morning, work breaks, and walking between sets at the gym
    (~[1:31:34]–[1:32:30]).
- **Don't jump straight to 10k.** If you walk 2k now, step up through 3k, 4k, 5k and onwards (~[1:32:30]–[1:32:52]).
- Don't link steps to the food-tracking app. The maintenance estimate already counts daily activity, so linking them
  counts it twice (~[1:32:54]–[1:33:44]).
- Hit protein on rest days too, since rest days are when muscle is built (~[1:03:02]–[1:03:14]).
- This lesson has no sleep rules.

#### Habits and mindset that could become small daily quests
- The foundation ("big domino") habits take about 10–15 min a day (~[00:24]).
- He puts fitness at roughly 20 % strategy and 80 % psychology (habits, motivation, beliefs) (~[01:52]).
- Progress, not perfection: climb one level at a time, ban no foods, just set limits (~[13:14]–[13:46],
  ~[16:17]–[16:27]).
- Plan meals a week ahead, the night before, or at least before ordering, and log them in advance. Without a plan,
  stress pushes you toward whatever food is nearest (~[1:17:58]–[1:18:45]).
- The weekly review produces reminders that show up on next week's tracker. From week 2, daily calorie and protein
  targets join the tracked habits (~[1:40:03]–[1:40:34]).
- When motivation drops, check the foundation habits first (rereading goals, routine, self-tracking, accountability,
  community) before changing the plan (~[1:37:35]–[1:38:17]).
- Rewatch the lesson and review its notes; repetition is how the ideas stick (~[1:39:05]–[1:39:46]).
- Aim for 1 % better each day; small daily actions add up (~[1:41:53]–[1:42:30]).

#### Nutrition (short)
- Calories decide whether weight goes up or down, and no food is "good" or "bad" for weight. "Healthy" foods can
  still be calorie-heavy (dressings, nut butters, avocado, poke or açaí bowls) (~[38:34]–[45:43]).
- Maintenance calories: take one estimate (a calculator or the food app) and accept that it's a guess. After 1–2 weeks,
  adjust by about 200 kcal based on what the scale does. If you already know your intake, don't recalculate. Cut
  first if you carry belly fat, are skinny-fat or aren't sure; bulk first if you're skinny. Start a bulk at +100–200
  kcal and a cut at about −500 (~[46:07]–[51:56]).
- Protein matters most of the three macros: 0.8–1 g per lb of bodyweight (160 lb → 128–160 g), every day. People over
  about 200 lb use a height-based chart instead. Carbs and fat can vary freely. If you can't hit both targets,
  calories come first; ending the day about 20 g short of protein is fine (~[56:31]–[1:03:10], ~[1:06:55]–[1:07:37]).
- Meal timing, meal frequency and intermittent fasting barely matter. Calories can be balanced across a week, but
  protein can't (~[1:05:40]–[1:06:48], ~[1:19:55]–[1:21:37]). Newcomers do best with fixed meal plans or a meal
  service for about 4 weeks while still logging, then gradually free up one or two meals a day (~[1:15:19]–[1:17:22]).
- Supplements only help you hit protein, give energy or cover vitamins; none burns fat or builds muscle by itself.
  Named: whey, creatine (5 g a day, barely noticeable effect), multivitamin, and caffeine, pre-workout and fat burners
  for energy (~[1:33:52]–[1:36:19]).

#### New vs already known
- **New:** firmer frequency guidance (4 a week is ideal, 5 adds little, 2 is a fallback, start at 2 and add); a step
  ramp of +1k at a time from the player's current level, with steps split into blocks; "lifting is for muscle, so one
  program fits every goal"; the list of low-value techniques; filming sets to check form; rest-day options. Cardio is
  explicitly optional, and HIIT and circuits are discouraged.
- **Differences from §5:** bulk surplus here is +100–200 kcal (§5 says about +100) and the cut starts at −500 (§5 says
  300–500). The bodyweight rule (every set to failure or near it) conflicts with §6.5's suggestion of no failure work
  for new players.

### 2. Week 3 – Calibrating and Fine Tuning: How to make adjustments to your Workout, Nutrition and Habits (2:05:30)

**Topic:** How to tell each week whether you're progressing (workout log, scale, tape measure), and what to change,
in what order, when you're not.

#### Training rules
- High habit scores alone won't change your body. The body adapts, so you have to keep adjusting (~[02:05]–[02:54]).
- Only three numbers drive decisions: gym performance (workout log), body weight (scale) and body-part measurements
  (tape measure). Photos and the mirror are a motivational bonus. Ignore body-fat and muscle-mass readings from smart
  scales and body-composition machines: they shift with water intake and sweat (~[04:34]–[08:21],
  ~[1:44:14]–[1:45:50], ~[1:51:17]–[1:55:36]).
- Steady strength gains are the simplest sign that muscle is growing (~[10:34]–[10:54]).
- To change any body part: pick the body part, pick exercises for it, then keep getting stronger on them with good
  form (more reps, more load or both, each week). Examples: chest → bench press, dumbbell flies, push-ups; glutes →
  squat, hip thrust, deadlift (~[12:20]–[14:17]).
- There are no magic rep counts, set counts, exercise orders, supersets or drop sets. A body part stops changing
  because you lift the same weight for the same reps for years. A few exercises that keep progressing beat many
  exercises done at the same load (~[15:01]–[18:38]).
- Progressive overload means more resistance over time, rising month to month if not week to week
  (~[19:03]–[19:45]).
- **Ways to progress that count** (smallest first, ~[20:09]–[22:20]):
  - same weight and reps with better form or range (for example half squat → full squat);
  - +1 rep at the same weight;
  - +1 lb at the same reps (micro-plates);
  - +1 set or +1 exercise.
- Form comes first: bad form misses the target muscle and risks injury, and an injury stops training altogether
  (~[20:25]–[21:12]).
- More isn't better. Extra sets and exercises are an advanced tool; too much volume prevents recovery and therefore
  progress (~[21:12]–[21:28]).
- But don't use "still fixing my form" as an excuse never to add weight. Solid form is enough; it doesn't have to be
  perfect (~[22:20]–[23:08]).
- Add reps and weight together when technique holds up. Once you're advanced and progress slows, pick one or alternate
  them (~[23:29]–[24:13]).
- When someone says a body part isn't changing, the first question is how much they lift on that exercise. Light loads
  usually mean they're new, aren't adding resistance, or don't know they should (~[24:40]–[26:30]).
- Log every workout in an app. Memory isn't reliable, and the log shows the numbers to beat next time
  (~[26:51]–[28:11]).
- You can't add something every session. Going backwards on some days or exercises is normal; what matters is that you
  keep trying (~[30:36]–[31:12]).
- What affects progress: sleep, form, intensity (too little gives no stimulus, too much leaves no recovery), stress
  from the rest of life, calories and protein (a deficit leaves fewer resources), and plain bad days
  (~[31:14]–[32:35]).
- Getting stronger in the gym is the main driver. Protein, calories, sleep, form and volume support it
  (~[32:50]–[36:55], ~[39:50]–[40:39]).
- Judge muscle loss by the strength trend over 3–4 weeks, not by one workout (~[44:38]–[45:46]). If lifts are rising,
  you're keeping muscle even though you look smaller on a cut. If they drop week after week, don't panic. Fix protein
  (aim for 1 g/lb), stop crash dieting, sleep more, clean up form, and stop piling on extra exercises and sets
  (~[46:20]–[47:41]).
- Want to add a set or an exercise? Treat it as a test: keep it if your lifts improve over the next few weeks, drop it
  if they fall (~[42:12]–[43:58]).
- "Beginner" means how long you've trained properly, not how long you've been going to a gym (~[1:04:16]–[1:04:41]).
- He rejects the "hardgainer" label. Slow progress, he says, comes from not adding resistance, not running the
  checklist below, not measuring and not adjusting (~[1:42:52]–[1:44:03]).

#### Cardio, steps, sleep and recovery
- Poor sleep lowers training energy and is listed as a factor in progress (~[31:18]–[31:25]); better sleep and rest
  speed up gym progress (~[40:25]–[40:31]).
- The same lesson also calls short sleep (5–6 h, even 3–4 h) acceptable as long as lifts keep rising
  (~[33:53]–[35:24], ~[41:46]–[41:55]). See the risky list.
- Steps are the "activity" item in the stall checklist: dropping from about 10k to 3k a day can explain a stall
  (~[1:10:56]–[1:11:38]).
- Adjusting a cut: eat 200–300 kcal less or add 2,000+ steps (~[1:30:34]–[1:30:56]). The cutting flowchart says
  100–200 kcal less, or more steps or cardio (~[1:33:37]). On a bulk that gains too slowly, eat more or walk less
  (~[1:03:20]–[1:03:30]).
- Don't react to a stall by suddenly doing punishing amounts of cardio (~[1:23:47]–[1:24:06]).

#### Habits and mindset that could become small daily quests
- Set a recurring reminder for photo and measurement days (~[00:04]–[00:28]).
- Weigh in daily at home: in the morning, after the bathroom, before eating or drinking, without shoes. Judge only the
  weekly average. Day-to-day swings come from water, salt, food volume and meal timing (~[50:51]–[56:03]).
- **Weekly four-question check ("WCAA")** before deciding you're stuck (~[1:07:07]–[1:12:37]). Fix any "no" first:
  - **Weigh-in:** daily, under the same conditions?
  - **Calories:** weekly average on target?
  - **Accuracy:** food tracked carefully, not guessed?
  - **Activity:** step target hit?
- Then check the tape. Signs of progress even when the scale is flat: the waist is shrinking; the waist holds while
  arms, chest, glutes or thighs grow; or both. On a cut it's normal for every measurement to shrink
  (~[1:16:12]–[1:20:11]).
- Measure every week (faster) or every 2 weeks (slower). The coach now recommends weekly, which allows an adjustment
  every week once the checklist and tape are done (~[1:21:12]–[1:22:31], ~[1:35:49]–[1:36:32]).
- In the weekly review, tick what to improve; ticked items come back as reminders on next week's tracker
  (~[1:12:42]–[1:13:33]). The weekly review is the data every adjustment is based on (~[1:59:09]–[1:59:23]).
- One-week challenge: get all four checklist items right for 7 days, even if that means eating the same reliable meals
  over and over (~[1:41:15]–[1:41:45]).
- The weekly loop: weigh in, train, eat to plan, walk, log habits → measure and take photos → adjust → repeat. The
  people who succeed are the ones who can put up with the boring repetition (~[1:57:40]–[1:59:05]).
- Keep what works, cut what doesn't (~[48:02]–[48:12]).
- Honesty rule: never tick a habit you didn't do (~[2:02:52]–[2:03:01]).
- Group tasks: go through the checklist with your accountability buddies and agree on a small penalty for missing it.
  Share something personal with the group to build trust (~[2:03:13]–[2:05:05]).
- Old habits have had decades to set; new ones have had weeks. Change has to be deliberate, and half effort gets half
  results (~[2:01:32]–[2:01:58], ~[2:05:14]).

#### Nutrition (short)
- Cutting pace: men 0.5–1 % of bodyweight a week, women 0.5–0.7 %. Faster risks losing muscle and strength
  (~[57:44]–[59:05]). Crash diets make the metabolism adapt, so weight loss stalls even at very low calories
  (~[1:00:49]–[1:02:03]).
- Lean bulk: about 0.5 % a week (1.5–2 % a month); women can go slower (under 1.5 % a month). A beginner can gain at
  most about 2 lb of muscle a month (men) or 1 lb (women), so a bigger surplus just adds fat (~[1:02:04]–[1:05:38]).
- Starting numbers are guesses (bulk +100–200 kcal, cut −500) (~[1:06:09]–[1:06:44]). If you aren't losing weight,
  you aren't in a deficit, whatever the calculator says. As you get lighter, your calorie needs drop
  (~[1:27:52]–[1:31:04], ~[1:32:20]–[1:32:46]).
- Order for a stall: run the checklist, then check the tape, then change calories or activity. A diet break is not
  the fix for a stall; it has a use, just not here (~[1:22:43]–[1:26:30], ~[1:31:11]–[1:32:59]).
- Abs show up once you lose enough weight. You don't need to know your body-fat percentage (~[1:54:28]–[1:54:48],
  ~[1:57:15]–[1:57:21]).

#### New vs already known
- **New:** the three-number model, with photos, the mirror and body-fat devices kept out of decisions; the list of
  small progressions that count (+1 rep, +1 lb, better range); solid form is enough to start adding load; the 3–4 week
  strength trend as the sign of muscle gain or loss; adding volume as a test you keep or drop; separate cutting paces
  for women (0.5–0.7 %/week); a fixed order for stalls with adjustment sizes (100–300 kcal or +2,000 steps); beginner
  defined by proper training time.
- **Partly contradicts §5:** §5 treats diet breaks as a general tool, while this lesson says a diet break is the wrong
  response to a stall. §5 has measurements every 2 weeks with weekly optional; this lesson pushes weekly. §5 treats
  sleep logging as core, while this lesson waves away short sleep as long as lifts rise.

### Anything risky for a beginner app

1. **Every set to failure on bodyweight moves** (decline push-ups, pull-ups). Week 2 ~[31:35]–[31:47]. This conflicts
   with §6.5. One all-out last set on a safe move is fine; failure on every set invites form breakdown in beginners.
2. **Short sleep and big deficits called fine if lifts rise.** Week 3 ~[33:53]–[35:24]: someone on a −800 kcal deficit
   sleeping 5–6 h is picked as the most likely to gain muscle. Week 3 ~[41:27]–[41:55]: 3–4 h of sleep, or eating about
   500 kcal under target for a couple of days, is called okay if progress continues. The app should never tell players
   that short sleep is acceptable.
3. **Supplement and medical claims.**
   - Fat burners, caffeine and pre-workout to fight diet fatigue: Week 2 ~[1:35:24]–[1:35:50].
   - Creatine at 5 g a day: Week 2 ~[1:35:00]–[1:35:20].
   - No upper limit on whey (3–4 scoops is fine): Week 2 ~[1:34:40]–[1:34:58].
   - High protein is safe for anyone without kidney disease: Week 2 ~[1:02:27]–[1:02:56].
   - Diet sodas are entirely safe: Week 2 ~[1:21:42]–[1:22:14].

   All of these are outside what a beginner app should advise.

### 8. 40 Fitness Facts That Will Shock You, Amaze You and Destroy The Myths You Still Think Are True (Week 12)

Source: `8. 40 Fitness Facts That Will Shock You, Amaze You and Destroy The Myths You Still Think Are True.txt`
(2:07:13). Whisper tagged it English, but most of it is machine-translated Taglish, and some passages are garbled
(noted inline). The speaker's numbering is loose: several items are bundled into one number.

**Topic in one line:** a myth-busting lesson on fat loss, protein, training methods, cardio, sweeteners and
supplements. Almost every answer comes back to calories, protein, progressive overload and good form.

#### Training rules
- Progressive overload, not soreness, shows that a workout works. The speaker built his physique while rarely being
  sore (~[23:07]–[24:11]).
- **Rep range:** moderate-to-heavy loads for about **6–15 reps** with progressive overload, whether cutting or bulking.
  High reps don't add "definition" and low reps aren't special for size. Definition comes from muscle size plus low
  body fat (~[38:39]–[40:52]).
- **Keep the same exercises** long enough to see whether you beat your previous numbers. Changing them every week or
  two ("muscle confusion") throws that away (~[41:00]–[42:30]).
- **There are no separate mass and toning exercises.** A muscle has one main action, so any variant that loads it
  works: barbell, dumbbell or cable, seated or standing. Compound lifts earn the "mass" label only because they work
  more muscles. Choose the variant you feel in the target muscle, can do with good form and enjoy, because that's the
  one you'll keep doing (~[1:06:22]–[1:14:46]). Muscle shape comes from genetics and body fat (~[1:11:14]–[1:11:20]).
- **Principles beat routines.** A celebrity routine or a bought 12-week plan fails if load and reps never rise or
  calories aren't set (~[1:14:58]–[1:20:43]).
- **Rest 2–3 min between sets.** Rests of 30–60 s cut the reps and load on later sets. Worked example: 100 lb × 10
  reps × 3 sets is 3,000 lb of volume; with 30 s rests it drops to about 1,850 lb (~[1:29:31]–[1:31:49],
  [1:35:22]–[1:36:15]). The transcript says "2–3 weeks" at [1:30:29]; minutes is clearly meant.
- **What drives muscle growth, ranked:** mechanical tension (a heavy-enough load plus overload) matters most;
  metabolic stress (the pump or burn) helps a little; muscle damage is a by-product (~[1:32:02]–[1:34:34]).
- **Knees past toes** is normal and safe in squats, lunges and split squats. Holding the knees back makes you lean
  forward and moves stress to the hips and back. The speaker cites about 20% less knee stress against roughly 1,070%
  more back stress; the original study measured hip torque, not the back (~[34:23]–[36:20]).
- With proper form, deep squats and deadlifts aren't bad for knees or back, and deep squats may strengthen the knees
  over time. Deadlifts are technical, so poor form raises the risk (~[37:28]–[38:35]).
- **Form first, belt later.** Learn proper form and use it for a few months before considering a belt. A belt only
  adds bracing pressure when you breathe into your belly against it. It doesn't fix bad form, and adding load to bad
  form raises injury risk (~[1:39:08]–[1:47:01]).
- **Men vs women:** the principles are the same. Lifting won't make women bulky because they have roughly a tenth of
  men's testosterone (~[1:28:53]–[1:29:26]). "Toning" simply means building muscle (~[1:32:16]–[1:32:44]).
- Body types (ectomorph, mesomorph, endomorph) don't need different training or diets, since everyone is a mix
  (~[49:38]–[53:50]).
- **Strength loss on a cut** (~[1:51:06]–[1:59:24]):
  - It can come from a bad day, poor sleep, an overreaching phase or high fatigue rather than muscle loss.
  - Leaner lifters lose some leverage (a longer range of motion), so a small drop is expected. This part is partly
    garbled (~[1:51:38]–[1:54:03]).
  - Acceptable: strength holds on most lifts, with an occasional loss of 1–3 reps or 2.5–10 lb on a big lift, while
    bodyweight keeps falling (~[1:54:20]–[1:54:45]).
  - Not acceptable: strength falls while weight doesn't, or one session drops by about 5 reps or 20 lb. That points
    to too steep a deficit, overtraining or skipped deloads. Judge the trend, not one session (~[1:57:04]–[1:57:55]).
  - Don't use any of this as an excuse to stop pushing (~[1:58:40]–[1:59:24]).
- Very lean, vascular physiques come from muscle plus low body fat and don't automatically mean steroids
  (~[1:36:24]–[1:39:02]).

#### Cardio, steps, sleep and recovery
- Fasted cardio is no better than fed cardio. A calorie-matched study of 20 women on a 500 kcal deficit found no
  difference, because the body evens out its fat use over the day (~[54:31]–[56:49]).
- **HIIT** (e.g. 20 s sprint, 40 s easy, for about 20 min) doesn't burn meaningfully more in total. The afterburn is
  about 80 extra kcal after a long, hard session, or roughly 40 kcal after a typical one (~[57:01]–[59:47]). HIIT only
  wins on time: 50–60 min of easy cardio matches or beats it (~[1:00:12]–[1:00:25]). It also adds fatigue that can
  hurt lifting and motivation (~[1:00:33]–[1:00:53]). Do the cardio you'll keep doing; the speaker sticks to walking
  (~[1:01:05]–[1:01:17]).
- You can't easily burn off a big meal. A 710 kcal burger is framed as an hour or two of running, or 25–30k steps,
  which is the speaker's rough and overstated estimate (~[26:44]–[27:16]).
- Sweating doesn't mean burning fat. Sauna suits only remove water weight (~[29:40]–[30:22]).
- When you stop training, muscle doesn't turn into fat. It shrinks, you burn fewer calories, and weight gain gets
  easier (~[24:13]–[25:12]).
- Poor sleep, overreaching and missed deloads are named as causes of falling strength (~[1:51:06]–[1:51:16],
  [1:57:40]–[1:57:55]).

#### Habits and mindset that could become small daily quests

  target by adjusting food or activity. Then do the weekly review that night (~[2:06:58]).
- **Myth check:** be sceptical of viral food-comparison images and documentaries, and look for credible rebuttals
  before changing your diet (~[12:13]–[12:25], [2:05:23]–[2:05:37]).
- **Progress metric:** use an estimated body-fat percentage and your own satisfaction rather than BMI
  (~[30:44]–[34:19]). The risky section has a caveat.
- If your abs don't show yet, the cause is body fat over them, not bad genetics. Keep going (~[1:59:25]–[2:01:33]).
- Avoid "shiny objects" such as body-type programs, celebrity routines and fat-burning foods (~[02:53], [1:26:20]–[1:28:37]).
- Act first, learn the theory second: knowledge without execution is useless (~[03:06]–[03:55]).

#### Nutrition

  foods, apple cider vinegar and lemon water. Organic, gluten-free and vegan diets aren't superior. Meal frequency,

### Week 6 - How To Strategically Eat More To Maximize Fat Loss And How To Use Fatigue

**Topic in one line.** Hunger and fatigue peak in cycles. Refeeds, diet breaks, one hard pre-deload week and then the
deload itself can turn that peak into progress (his simplified fitness-fatigue model).

**Training rules**
- Progressive overload means adding reps, load or sets from week to week (~[35:00]). Fatigue rises faster than
  fitness, so around weeks 5–6 lifts stall or slip. He calls this overreaching: a short-term drop in performance
  caused by whole-body (systemic) fatigue, not just tired muscles (~[35:26]–[38:24]).
- Signs of overreaching: low energy, general tiredness, struggling to get out of bed, low drive to train, feeling
  unwell, worse sleep, needing more coffee, slightly weaker lifts, mood swings, changed heart rate or blood pressure
  (~[38:30]). Caffeine, pre-workout and other stimulants can hide these signs (~[39:20]). The clearest sign is lifts
  stalling or dropping (~[39:43]).
- Judge fatigue as a trend over about a week, never from one day. That is why fatigue is logged daily
  (~[40:07]–[40:26]).
- His model is a 7-week block (mesocycle): weeks 1–5 normal overload, week 6 a deliberately hard week, week 7 deload.
  The next block (weeks 8–14) repeats with more load and volume (~[40:48], [43:17]–[44:31], [1:02:53]–[1:03:25]).
  The week-7 timing comes from his older programs and is not a fixed rule (~[48:32]–[48:52]).
- How often to deload (~[49:00]–[49:51]):
  - Beginner: less than about a year of *proper* training (knowing and applying overload), however many years
    you've been in a gym. Deload every 5–8 weeks.
  - Intermediate and beyond (over a year of proper training): every 4–6 weeks. Stronger lifters create more
    stress per session, but recovery only improves so far (~[50:08]–[51:16]).
  - The right interval differs a lot between people: about 4 weeks for some of his coaches, 8 for others
    (~[51:18], [54:25]–[54:43]). If unsure, follow the program's schedule and learn your own cycle from your
    workout log (~[51:56], [53:15]).
- Changing the timing (~[52:16]–[53:13], [1:19:43]–[1:20:30]):
  - If overreaching signs show up early (e.g. week 4–5), make that week the hard week and deload the next.
  - If you still feel fresh at the scheduled deload, keep going (he says possibly 2–3 more weeks) until signs or a
    plateau appear. Repeat the last week's workout (e.g. week 6) rather than going back to week 1.
  - Cycles are shorter when cutting and longer when bulking (~[53:31]–[54:01]). Big life stress (a breakup, job
    loss, family problems) also shortens them (~[54:01]–[54:15]).
- How to deload (~[54:47]–[56:38]):
  - Follow the program's own protocol. In his older programs, lower loads by 30–50 %. In the Kaizen Workout
    Builder, keep the loads from the week before the deload.
  - Drop 1–2 sets per exercise (4 → 2, 3 → 2), abs included.
  - Drop 1–3 reps, depending on the exercise.
  - Cut steps or cardio by 20–30 %.
  - When the next block starts, begin the top load one step lower (his example: 100 lb before the deload, 90–95 lb
    after) so there is room to build momentum.
  - Aim to set at least one new personal record in every block before the deload (~[56:47]).
- Skipping deloads or doing them half-heartedly leads to overtraining: unusual muscle pain, injuries, big strength
  and muscle loss, getting sick, and weeks to months of rest to recover (~[57:09]–[58:47]).
- Deloading before you ever reach overreaching is also fine. It lowers plateau risk, but you miss the extra rebound
  (supercompensation). He recommends it for people who can't read their own fatigue signals: just deload every
  5–8 weeks (~[58:59]–[1:00:37]).
- The hard week before a deload (he calls it "hell week") (~[1:01:22]–[1:12:33]):
  - It carries the most risk: ego lifting, sloppy form, cheated reps and injury. Before it starts, set an
    intention to keep strict form and not ego-lift (~[1:01:51]).
  - Intensity techniques. Pick 1–3, not all of them. Wanting to do all of them means you aren't tired enough yet
    (~[1:10:18]–[1:10:48]). The options:
    - Sets to failure (RPE 10, 0 reps in reserve): you could not do one more rep with proper form
      (~[1:03:48], [1:05:50]–[1:06:52]).
    - Add 1–2 sets (~[1:07:56]).
    - Add an extra exercise for the body part you want to grow, 2–3 sets (~[1:08:05]).
    - Supersets and drop sets, which he calls safer than straight failure (~[1:08:16]–[1:09:08]). Superset
      example: bench press until another rep is unlikely, re-rack, then push-ups to failure. Drop-set example:
      lunges with 40 lb dumbbells, then 25 lb dumbbells to failure.
    - "Finisher" sets from a paid add-on (not explained) (~[1:09:18]).
    - One extra training day that week (3 → 4, 4 → 5) (~[1:09:41]).
  - Never take squats or bench press to failure without safety pins or racks, or a spotter (~[1:05:21]–[1:05:40]).
  - Never use these techniques outside the week before a deload. Training all-out from week 1 only causes early
    burnout and no progress (~[1:11:05]–[1:12:08]).
  - Losing some strength that week is expected (~[1:12:11]).
- Some fatigue is left over after each deload and builds up across blocks. After 3–5 blocks, plan a lighter
  "maintenance phase": an extended deload with lower volume and load for a whole block, roughly every 4–6 months
  (2–3 times a year), to avoid burnout (~[47:09]–[48:24]).
- Men vs women: no difference in the training rules. Only the promised outcome is framed differently (bigger
  muscles for men, a more toned look for women) (~[42:30]).

**Cardio, steps, sleep and recovery**
- In the deload week, cut steps or cardio by 20–30 % (~[55:44]).
- Worse sleep and needing more coffee are signs of overreaching (~[38:42]).
- Recovery is better when eating more (bulking) and worse in a deficit or under life stress (~[53:31]–[54:15]).
- If you use pre-workout, he suggests saving it for the hard week and stopping during the deload so you don't build
  a tolerance (~[1:12:36]).
- Expect a second and third motivation dip around weeks 13 and 20, driven by peak fatigue and hunger, even when
  habits are strong (~[04:45]–[06:10], [11:51]–[12:20]).

**Habits and mindset that could become small daily quests**
- A daily fatigue log, reviewed as a weekly trend (~[40:19]).
- A one-line "form first, no ego lifting" intention before each session of the hard week (~[1:01:51]).
- Treat a stall just before a deload as expected rather than a failure; quitting at this point is the common mistake
  (~[45:44]–[47:01]).
- Sunday planning: put refeed and deload dates on the calendar in advance, so there is something to look forward to
  (~[1:20:36]–[1:20:49]).
- Commit to not missing sessions. Half effort gives half results and knocks on to other habits
  (~[1:21:09]–[1:21:25]).
- Before a focus block (here, the lesson itself), state an intention to stay focused to the end (~[01:37]–[01:51]).

**Nutrition**
- A refeed is 2 days in a row at estimated maintenance or slightly above. The extra calories come mostly from carbs
  (extra kcal ÷ 4 = grams of carbs; 600 kcal → 150 g). Protein stays at 0.8–1 g per lb, and fat stays the same or
  drops to make room (~[13:35]–[16:02], [20:25]–[24:58]).

- The scale going up after a refeed is water and food volume, not fat. He cites a surplus of about 3,500 kcal per lb
  of fat (~[27:42]–[31:11]).
- Hunger tools: eat slowly, brush your teeth after eating, a 10-minute rule from Week 4 (not explained here), more
  protein (whey when hungry), more vegetables (~[12:38]–[13:18]).

### Week 8 - How To Perform Even Better In The Next 50 Days And Smash Your 100th Day Physique (S3)

**Topic in one line.** A day-50 recap, replayed from an earlier season with a note that women's targets differ:
- focus on what you control;
- adjust every week when the numbers stall;
- stop worrying about muscle loss while lifts keep rising;
- simplify food and cut distractions;
- run a deeper Sunday weekly review.

**Training rules**
- On a cut, gym progress is the main sign that you are keeping or gaining muscle. If load or reps keep rising with
  proper form, you aren't losing muscle, even if your chest, arms or shoulders look smaller (they carry fat too)

- Only conclude you're losing muscle when lifts fall week after week, never from one bad session
  (~[40:26]–[40:43]).
- If lifts are trending down, check protein, the size of the deficit (losing ~2 % of bodyweight a week is too fast)
  and sleep (~[41:06]–[41:28]).
- Things you control are the inputs: workouts, calories, protein and the daily step target. The gym log, scale and
  tape are signals. How you look changes last (lead vs lagging indicators) (~[05:41]–[06:35], [09:09]–[09:34]).
- Don't switch to a new program at the midway dip if the current one is working. Repeat it and do it better
  (~[1:08:12]–[1:11:40]).
- He only gives physique-photoshoot tips to people at about 10 % body fat (~[1:02:20]).
- Men vs women: no training differences. Only the weight-loss target differs (see Nutrition).

**Cardio, steps, sleep and recovery**
- Steps are one of the two main adjustment levers:
  - +500–1,000 a day when loss is slow;
  - +1,000–2,000 when it's stuck (~[21:37]–[21:52], [24:05]–[24:11], [32:13]–[32:31]);

  consistently (~[1:19:19]).
- Poor sleep is one of the first things to check when lifts drop (~[41:19]).

**Habits and mindset that could become small daily quests**
- In the morning, do the low-stimulation tasks first (reading, meditating, planning, the workout). Phone, social
  media and streaming come after; if you can't drop them, push them to the evening (~[56:34]–[57:00]).
- Keep the phone away from the bed and wake up with a physical alarm clock (~[1:18:17], [1:19:57]).
- Don't keep tempting foods at home; clear them out at the start of the week (~[1:18:29], [1:20:04]).
- Step away from online arguments (politics and the like); they drain focus (~[57:15]–[57:53]).

  1. List every win (to train yourself to notice the good).
  2. What did I want to happen last week?
  3. What actually happened, and why?
  4. What were the biggest obstacles?
  5. What lessons did I learn?
  6. Any regrets or frustrations?
  7. What worked, and what didn't?
  8. Which 3–5 things would make this week better than the last?

  (~[1:20:31]–[1:20:54], [1:26:07]–[1:26:56]). Reread the notes during the week (~[1:21:39]).

- Fat comes off in a different order for everyone; often the last place you gained is the first to go. Don't judge
  progress by one body part (~[34:12]–[35:28]).

  strengthen the accountability group (~[58:33]–[1:01:15]).

  (~[01:29]–[01:59], [23:12]–[23:31]).

### Week 13 - Bulk & Cut Masterclass Pt. 1

**Topic in one line.** Why fat loss and muscle gain should run as separate phases that alternate (cut, then bulk,
repeated over years), and the mindset blocks that stop people doing it.

**Training rules**
- Muscle builds up across many cycles through steady progressive overload. The awkward early look (lean but thin after
  a first cut, soft after a first bulk) goes away only as muscle accumulates (~[40:38]–[41:09]).
- Train hardest during the calorie-surplus phase, because that is when gym work turns into new muscle. In a deficit,
  new muscle is unlikely except for true beginners; losing fat mostly reveals muscle that was already there
  (~[49:02]–[49:25]).
- More sessions are not the lever. The coach describes years of training 5–6 days a week with poor results until he
  fixed his eating (~[07:22]–[07:42]). This supports the known advice of 3–4 days a week.
- Program-hopping is a beginner trap: he used to switch every month to a new magazine routine (supersets, drop sets
  and other techniques), each time believing it was the missing piece (~[54:20]–[54:51]). Stick to the basics and do
  them consistently (~[02:08]–[02:17], ~[1:05:09]–[1:05:14]).
- The steady toolkit he names is to track calories, apply progressive overload, keep the tracking checklist (WCAA) and
  make adjustments (~[56:11]–[56:15]).
- Once enough muscle is built, maintaining it takes less work. After about 15 years he still trains, walks and eats
  carefully, just less strictly (~[1:01:21]–[1:01:57]).
- Men vs women: the same concepts apply to women. He used only male examples by choice (~[37:46]–[38:17]).

**Cardio, steps, sleep and recovery**
- Piling on cardio during a bulk to stop fat gain is a mistake, because it means trying to bulk and cut at the same
  time (~[34:17]–[34:23], ~[36:05]–[36:10]).
- Daily steps stay a lifelong habit even for him (~[1:01:31]).
- The video gives no sleep or recovery rules.

**Habits and mindset that could become small daily quests**
- Accept that progress is slow. Doing the boring basics consistently turns out to be the fastest route
  (~[01:44]–[02:17], ~[1:20:47]).

  urgency and leads to delays and lapses (~[22:27]–[23:27], ~[31:35]–[31:42]). *Quest idea: one small action today
  instead of postponing.*
- Think in years, not months. Fitness is lifelong and matters more with age (~[03:53]–[04:02],
  ~[1:00:36]–[1:00:43], ~[1:06:41]–[1:06:53]). *Quest idea: a yearly or quarterly "where am I going" reflection.*

  Quitting means rebuilding the same habits from zero later (~[1:10:16]–[1:10:32]).

  (~[1:20:38]–[1:20:42], ~[1:21:40]). The weekly review is already known.

**Nutrition**
- You cannot out-train a bad diet. Calories in vs calories out decides the result (~[12:01]–[12:16]).
- Run the deficit and the surplus as separate phases that alternate. Don't settle for half-measures at maintenance

### Week 14 - Bulk & Cut Masterclass Pt. 2

**Topic in one line.** The practical how-to of bulking, cutting and maintenance: who starts with which, how fast, how
long, body-fat ranges, transitions, common mistakes, and why "recomp" should not be the goal.

**Training rules**
- Gym progress (getting stronger on your logged exercises, i.e. progressive overload) is the best sign of muscle
  gained or lost. Arm size, how clothes fit and the mirror are not (~[59:04]–[59:15], ~[1:24:38]–[1:24:55]).
- Bulking: the priority order is gym progress first, eating a bit more as support, and weight gain only as a side
  effect. Judge a bulk by how much stronger you got, not by the kilos gained (~[1:36:08]–[1:39:23]).
- Cutting: the priority is fat loss, and strength and muscle are secondary. Holding strength, or losing a few reps
  (1–3) or a little load, is acceptable; keep lifting anyway (~[03:56]–[05:06], ~[47:27]–[48:07],
  ~[1:25:46]–[1:26:02]).
- Keep lifting, progressive overload and protein during a cut. With those in place any muscle lost is small and comes
  back quickly once calories rise (~[04:13]–[04:18], ~[48:31]–[48:45]).
- Recovery and volume depend on the diet phase. In a deficit you handle less volume, overreaching comes sooner and the
  deload comes earlier. In a surplus you recover faster, handle more volume, and the training block can run longer
  before overreaching (~[44:48]–[45:03], ~[49:09]–[49:31]).
- Beginners can build some muscle even in a deficit, because they respond strongly to training (~[02:52]–[03:00],
  ~[12:46]–[13:04]). As training age rises, a surplus becomes necessary for new muscle (~[1:01:45]–[1:01:55],
  ~[1:49:45]–[1:50:08]).
- Realistic muscle gain: at most about 2 lb (0.9 kg) a month for men and 1 lb (0.45 kg) for women with perfect
  execution, and more realistically 0.5–1 lb (~[16:21]–[16:31], ~[25:20]–[25:40]).
- Muscle gain also depends on progressive overload, rest, sleep and enough calories (~[19:04]–[19:14]).
- Shrinking on a cut (smaller shirt size, slimmer arms, chest or glutes) is mostly lost fat, not lost muscle
  (~[45:27]–[46:17]).
- Men vs women: different body-fat ranges and weekly loss targets (see Nutrition). The principles are the same.

**Cardio, steps, sleep and recovery**
- Sign of metabolic adaptation: about 1,000 kcal a day plus 15,000+ steps, with fat still to lose but no weight loss.
  The fix is a diet break, not more restriction (~[1:32:07]–[1:32:38]).
- Diet breaks: 2–4 weeks at maintenance after the 100-day program (~[1:32:53]–[1:33:05]). Experienced dieters take
  7 days at maintenance every 6–7 weeks of continuous dieting (~[1:34:22]–[1:34:52]). Breaks come more often (about
  every 4 weeks) when intake is already very low (around 900 kcal) but body fat is still high (~[1:35:06]–[1:35:28]).
  A diet break means maintenance calories, not a free-for-all (~[1:35:39]–[1:35:57]).
- Between phases, a 2–4 week maintenance stretch is recommended but optional. After a cut it lets hunger settle and
  prevents binges; after a bulk it avoids a sudden calorie drop (~[53:14]–[54:26], ~[55:58]–[56:58]).
- Very deep cuts bring tiredness, high hunger, mood swings and irritability (~[49:39]–[49:44]). At contest-level
  leanness energy is so low that people rely on caffeine and pre-workout to function (~[38:00]–[38:36]); see the risky
  section.

**Habits and mindset that could become small daily quests**
- Choose one goal (bulk or cut), see it through, then follow through on the other phase (~[1:06:25]–[1:07:21]).
- Don't mark the end of a cut with a binge. A planned refeed or diet break is fine (~[1:10:01]–[1:10:38],
  ~[1:17:27]–[1:17:53]). *Quest idea: a planned "finish line" meal instead of an open-ended celebration.*
- Track lead indicators (weigh-ins, tape measure, the tracking checklist) rather than lag indicators (how you look);
  the look follows later (~[1:48:04]–[1:48:33]). The mirror and photos change day to day with water and bloating, so
  they are poor weekly guides (~[1:46:07]–[1:46:31]).
- Plan the coming year's phases ahead of time (~[2:00:11]–[2:00:20]). *Quest idea: a yearly planning reflection.*
- After finishing a challenge, stop and actually celebrate and feel proud (~[1:59:30]–[1:59:51]). *Quest idea: a
  milestone moment when a block or challenge is completed.*
- Be honest about whether you are truly satisfied with your body or just avoiding effort (~[05:56]–[07:07]).
- Weekly review on the lesson night (~[2:00:52]) is already known.
- *Quest idea (training): a weekly "strength check" comparing this week's logged reps or load with last week's.*

**Nutrition**
- Who starts with what: skinny and lean people bulk first. People with fat to lose (obese, overweight or skinny-fat)
  cut first. If you are unsure, cut first: you are probably a skinny-fat beginner, fat loss shows quicker wins, and
  bulking works better from lean. Maintain only if you are genuinely satisfied, or on purpose as a diet break
  (~[05:10]–[07:24], ~[09:08]–[15:20]).
- Bulk slowly: +100–200 kcal a day (worked example about 166 kcal), or about 0.5 % of bodyweight a week (1.5–2 % a
  month). Bulk for at least 4 months, ideally 6+, with 4–6 week mini-cuts if fat builds up. Fast or "dirty" bulks
  mostly add fat (~[15:31]–[16:00], ~[17:24]–[17:37], ~[25:01]–[25:08], ~[29:09]–[31:27], ~[1:17:55]–[1:20:38]).
- Cut faster: about 0.7 % of bodyweight a week for women and 1 % for men, up to about 0.9 % and 1.2 % with high body
  fat. Around 2 % a week raises the risk of muscle loss and adaptation. A big first-week drop is mostly water, so wait
  2–3 weeks before adjusting (~[19:36]–[20:16], ~[22:27]–[23:05]). Rule of thumb: 1 lb of fat is about 3,500 kcal, so
  500 kcal a day below maintenance is about 1 lb a week (~[21:18]–[22:08]). A typical cut lasts 2–6 months with
  refeeds and diet breaks; with a lot to lose, cut until done (~[31:39]–[32:43]).
- Cycle by body fat in roughly 6-point bands: men cut to about 10–12 % and bulk to about 16–18 %; women cut to about
  18–20 % and bulk to about 24–26 %. Judge it by mirror and pinch, not by measurement (~[34:07]–[36:31],
  ~[39:36]–[40:14], ~[35:14]–[35:23], ~[1:56:41]–[1:57:18]).
- At a transition, don't recompute calories with online calculators; adjust from your current intake and your own
  logged data (~[57:05]–[58:23]). Don't make recomposition the goal; treat it as a bonus (~[1:42:09]–[1:51:51]).

**New vs already known**

### 9. How to avoid this type of diet that delays your progress by over a year & makes it 100x harder for you to lose weight (Week 15)

**Topic in one line.** Metabolic adaptation: repeated crash-diet and binge cycles (yo-yo dieting) push body fat up
while lowering the calories you can eat. How to prevent or repair it with diet breaks, refeeds and reverse dieting,
plus progress-photo tips.

**Training rules**
- Never stop training when you stop dieting. Eating more while not training loses muscle and lowers metabolism even
  further; he calls this the biggest mistake and his own past habit of only training from January until summer
  (~[42:09]–[43:09]). Keep training for life (~[43:03]–[43:09]).
- Build and keep muscle through workouts with progressive overload. More muscle raises the calories you can eat
  (~[42:01]–[42:05], ~[42:44]–[42:50]).
- Don't start at the hardest level; go level 1, then level 2. He applies this to dieting too, referring back to
  Week 2 (~[17:12]–[17:24]).
- As you get lighter, the same lift (his example: a 20 kg squat) burns fewer calories, because the body becomes more
  efficient. The numbers are illustrative (~[25:19]–[26:15]).
- Pre-photo pump: light weights for 12–20 reps on the body parts you want to show, not to failure, because muscle
  damage and inflammation blur definition (~[1:40:38]–[1:41:04]).
- Otherwise the video has no set, rep or frequency rules.

**Cardio, steps, sleep and recovery**
- As weight drops, everyday movement (fidgeting, gestures, walking pace) falls without you noticing, and the same
  10,000 steps burns less. His illustration: about 400 kcal falling to 200–300 kcal after losing 20–30 lb
  (~[22:42]–[24:16]).
- Two people of the same height and weight walking the same steps burn different amounts, so don't compare step
  results (~[24:20]–[24:50]).
- More cardio is not the answer. His contrast: a dieter on 10–15k steps plus 1–2 h of cycling 3 times a week who
  needs 1,200 kcal to lose weight, versus the coach on 6–8k steps, no cardio and 2,400–2,600 kcal. He puts the
  difference down to dieting history (~[01:52]–[02:56]).
- Diet-break schedule (all at maintenance calories):
  - First long cut: after 12–14 weeks of continuous dieting, take 2–4 weeks (~[52:31]–[52:48]).
  - Later cuts: 1 week every 6–7 weeks, which he equates with one training block or "mesocycle" (~[53:06]–[53:19]).
  - Slow-metabolism cases (about 1,000–1,200 kcal with lots of fat left): every 4–6 weeks (he suggests 4), for 2–3
    weeks (~[53:25]–[53:53]).
  - He cites research that 2–4 weeks at maintenance reverses the effects of underfeeding (~[55:04]–[55:18]).
- How many breaks depends on body fat. Above about 20 % (men) or 30 % (women), cut at the target rate with frequent
  refeeds and diet breaks. Below that, use fewer breaks (mostly refeeds) and finish the cut quickly
  (~[50:36]–[51:06], ~[56:24]–[57:26]).
- At maintenance, weight rises a little from water, then stabilizes (~[47:28]–[47:41], ~[58:23]–[58:40]).
- Stress makes the body hold water, so stay relaxed on photo day (~[1:41:10]–[1:41:18]).
- Refeeds: every 3 weeks when you consistently hit the deficit and lose weight each week. This refers back to the
  Week 6 slide and is not fully spoken here (~[52:06]–[52:24]).

**Habits and mindset that could become small daily quests**
- Treat fitness as part of who you are, not something you do now and then (~[01:00]–[01:19]).
- Drop "January or Monday instead": each binge-and-restart cycle makes the next diet harder (~[17:49]–[18:06],
  ~[1:58:53]–[1:59:03]).
- After an overeating day, get back on track in the following days rather than letting it run for weeks
  (~[1:18:48]–[1:18:56]). *Quest idea: "back on plan" the next day, with no penalty.*
- Log everything you eat and drink, including bites and sips. Keep meals fairly consistent; guessing at a different
  eatery every day makes logs unreliable (~[1:02:05]–[1:03:12]). *Quest idea: a "log everything today" day.*
- Before deciding you have a slow metabolism, check the basics: daily weigh-ins, consistent and accurate food and
  activity logs, and body measurements (~[1:01:08]–[1:01:32]).
- Habits are shaped largely by environment and triggers. Keep reminders around you (weekly takeaways, sharing wins,
  accountability meetings, seeing others' progress) and like-minded people. Old circles trigger old habits
  (~[1:47:44]–[1:48:34], ~[1:48:58]–[1:49:06]). Bad habits took decades to form, so dropping the good ones after 100
  days invites relapse (~[1:46:51]–[1:47:07]).
- When a program ends, take a diet break and keep going (~[1:59:03]).
- Progress photos: in the final stretch, take them daily under different conditions (after waking, after a workout,
  after eating) and keep the photos you like. Use your usual poses, the same setting and outfit as day 1 if possible,
  and no oil or lotion (~[1:37:29]–[1:37:43], ~[1:39:15]–[1:40:30]).
- Patience is the skill that makes the slow fixes work (~[49:49], ~[1:16:10]).

**Nutrition**
- In a yo-yo cycle (crash diet, then weeks of binging, then a stricter diet), each round ends with more body fat and
  lower maintenance calories. Metabolism adapts down over months but recovers slowly, and short binges barely raise it
  (~[04:19]–[14:10]). He says the graph numbers are made up (~[04:38]).
- What drops is basal burn, everyday movement, exercise burn and the energy used to digest food (~[19:46]–[27:12]).
  Fast overfeeding may add fat cells, possibly permanently (attributed to Lane Norton) (~[27:58]–[30:09]). Online
  calculators ignore dieting history, so base changes on your current intake (~[38:57]–[39:55]).
- To limit the slowdown: avoid yo-yo dieting, keep lifting, avoid crash diets and multi-day fasts (keep any aggressive
  phase short), eat 0.8–1 g of protein per lb, don't stay in a cut too long, don't binge at the cut-to-bulk
  transition, and use refeeds, diet breaks and reverse dieting (~[41:53]–[45:16]).
- If you are stuck at very low calories after checking the basics, make one last 200–300 kcal reduction, then stop
  cutting and reverse diet for 4–12+ months (~[1:03:45]–[1:07:08]). The signs: about 1,000 kcal, 15k+ steps, fat
  still to lose, no loss (~[1:22:05]–[1:22:32]). Method: maintenance for 2–4 weeks, then add 50–100 kcal every 1–2
  weeks, only while weight stays stable (~[1:23:43]–[1:24:20]). A bulk adds 100–150 kcal at a plateau and aims for
  +0.5 % a week; a reverse diet adds 50–100 and aims to hold weight (~[1:22:38]–[1:23:31]).
- Results vary: some people raise maintenance by only 200–300 kcal, others by 500+ (~[1:19:08]–[1:19:24]). Cited data
  (Lane Norton's book): 55 clients went from about 1,500 to about 2,200 kcal on average while gaining about 1.6 kg
  (~[1:20:24]–[1:20:56]).

**New vs already known**
- New: the mechanism (everyday movement and other burn falling); "don't stop training when the diet ends"; checking

  cycle equals "one mesocycle" of 6–7 weeks, which matches the builders' 6-week block (my observation, not theirs).

- Unclear: ~[1:08:41]–[1:08:47] ("1000 to 111 calories") and ~[1:11:30] ("1817") are garbled numbers. At

- *Daily routine,* about 10–15 minutes in total (~[1:30:23]–[1:30:28]). The steps match §5, but the reasons are new:
  - Writing the day's commitments sets intention (~[1:28:00]–[1:28:10]).
  - Planning tomorrow (meals, workout time and goals, groceries) hands decisions to your logical self instead of
    your mood (~[1:28:44]–[1:29:21]).
  - Wins train gratitude (~[1:29:22]–[1:29:38]).
  - "One thing to do better tomorrow" stops mistakes snowballing (~[1:29:42]–[1:30:00]).
- *Weekly routine has three parts:* scoring, review and planning, and an accountability meeting
  (~[1:31:57]–[1:32:05]). The Sunday review asks for wins, challenges, scores, what to adjust and what to change; do
  more of what works (~[1:34:14]–[1:35:31]). Correct course weekly, not after 50 days. A quick 5 kg gain takes weeks
  to undo (~[1:34:35]–[1:36:17]).
- *Weekly meeting agenda (6 steps):*
  1. Report last week's commitment.
  2. Discuss your habit score.
  3. Make a new commitment.
  4. Ask the group for tips and share what works.
  5. Do the group action items.
  6. Close by pushing each other for a higher score.
  (~[1:37:11]–[1:40:28]). App version: a weekly "commit, then report" loop with an opt-in buddy.
- *Routine as the fallback.* When life is messy (a breakup, business trouble, no drive), do the mini-habits first,
  then deal with the problem from a calmer state (~[1:48:23]–[1:49:33]). It works on any schedule, including travel,
  because everyone wakes and sleeps (~[1:49:43]–[1:50:21]).
- *Honesty.* Tick only what you actually did; faked scores make coaching impossible (~[1:33:05]–[1:33:14],
  [2:04:24]–[2:04:58]).
- *Big setup tasks* (Week 1 action items take about 2 hours): do them while motivation is high, one item at a time
  to avoid overwhelm (~[2:08:36]–[2:10:19]).
- Watch the lesson at a fixed, distraction-free time early in the week (~[2:11:06]–[2:11:52]). Update the tracker
  daily, not at the deadline, because rushed updates come out half-done (~[2:12:59]–[2:13:22]). Feeling overwhelmed
  in week 1 is normal (~[2:13:31]–[2:14:03]).

**Nutrition**
- The physical basics are simple: a calorie deficit or surplus, gym training, and enough protein
  (~[11:54]–[12:03]).
- A single cut (for example, one less cup of rice) won't work forever; intake has to be adjusted as the body adapts
  (~[19:00]–[19:18]).
- Bulking is treated as being as hard as cutting, and is now judged and rewarded in the challenge too
  (~[1:55:47]–[1:56:05]).

**New vs already known**
- New compared with §5:
  - the five-step self-score;
  - the scoring mechanics (points per habit per day, weekly total, "score to beat");
  - the four accountability levels and the 6-step meeting agenda;
  - the three self-sabotaging thoughts with reframes;
  - morning and evening counted from your own wake and sleep times;
  - the reasons behind each routine step.
- No contradiction with §5. The punitive side (red flag below 75, three strikes and you're out) is new and listed
  under risks below.

### Week 5 - Hacking Your Motivation- How to reprogram yourself to reach goals faster and easier!

**Topic in one line.** Motivation always drops (the "dip" after roughly the first month), so rely on habits, daily
rereading of goals and identity statements, and hacks that add reasons to keep going or remove reasons to stop.

**Training rules**
- *Deload:* a planned easy week after roughly 6 weeks of pushing, to clear the fatigue that drags down mood, energy
  and motivation. Details were deferred to Week 6 (~[1:06:56]–[1:07:53]).
- *Rest between sessions:* with 3 sessions a week, train Monday, rest Tuesday, train Wednesday (~[1:07:25]–[1:07:37]).
- *Minimum version when ill or exhausted:* keep 3 sessions a week but do 1 set per exercise, very light
  (~[1:10:20]–[1:10:28]), or drop to 2 sessions a week (~[1:10:49]–[1:10:51]). Returning to full training took 2–4
  weeks, and was easy because the habit was kept alive (~[1:10:31]–[1:10:36]).
- *"No zero days" floor:* on the worst days, 5 push-ups and 5 bodyweight squats count as the workout tick
  (~[1:13:04]–[1:13:21]).
- *Fatigue and the dip:* rising fatigue from weeks of consistent training is one cause of the motivation dip
  (~[10:03]–[10:14]).
- *Form checks* are listed as a way to stay engaged (~[1:03:35]–[1:03:39]).
- *Progressive overload* is named as the gym rule and then borrowed as a mindset for habit scores
  (~[1:01:33]–[1:02:28]).

**Cardio, steps, sleep and recovery**
- *Steps floor when sick:* about 1,000 or even 500 steps instead of 10k (~[1:10:10]–[1:10:13], [1:13:13]).
- *Coming back after a break:* start very small and add about 1 % a day. Example: 500 steps for a few consistent
  days, then 600, then 700 (~[1:11:27]–[1:11:46]).
- The coach journals sleep, energy, mood, focus, fatigue, stress, wake time, work hours and bedtime. His motivation
  peaked on Monday and fell through the week; his aim was to make every day feel like Monday (~[02:43]–[04:12]).
- The deload is described as recovery on a weekly scale, the way a rest day is recovery on a daily scale
  (~[1:07:19]–[1:07:46]).

**Habits and mindset that could become small daily quests**
- *The motivation curve* (~[06:46]–[18:51]):
  - An "inspired phase" lasts about 4 weeks, varying by person (~[08:05]–[08:19]).
  - Then a dip. The causes: you see the real effort involved, the novelty wears off, training fatigue builds,
    hunger rises on a diet, and slip-ups feel like total failure (~[08:58]–[10:38]).
  - Habit strength climbs slowly underneath. The low point, where effort shows no visible result yet, is where
    most people quit (~[11:24]–[12:19]).
  - The breakthrough comes around days 50–66, and the lesson cites 66 days as the average to form a habit
    (~[14:57]–[15:17]).
  - After that, motivation keeps going up and down, but the habits carry you (~[16:04]–[17:19]).
  - Quitting resets habit strength to zero and trains a habit of quitting (~[17:47]–[18:51]).
  - App takeaway: a "you're in the dip, this is normal" message around weeks 4–9, and a visible habit-strength meter
    that doesn't reset to zero after one miss.
- *Rule:* don't rely on motivation; build habits (~[19:19]–[19:37]).
- *Reflection quest:* recall a past project you quit. When did you quit (which week), and what did you tell
  yourself? Then write 2 or more commitments for when the dip hits (~[21:16]–[22:33]).
- *Two kinds of hack:* those that add reasons to keep going and those that remove reasons to stop
  (~[24:04]–[24:18]). Don't adopt them all at once; keep them as a toolbox (~[1:08:20]–[1:08:30]).
- *Daily rereading* of your goals, vision, identity worksheet and future-self letter, paired with implementation
  intentions (~[24:46]–[25:04], [48:35]–[49:22]).
- *Identity statements.* Short "I am someone who…" lines (never misses a workout, always logs food), read daily.
  The claim: once rehearsed, acting against that self-image feels uncomfortable and nudges you back on track (he
  calls this cognitive dissonance) (~[43:03]–[47:42]). Expect an inner critic to argue at first; keep repeating
  (~[41:19]–[41:53]).
- *Do the boring basics again and again* instead of chasing a new program (~[49:33]–[49:47]).
- *Process over outcome.* The outcome might be "lose 10 lb"; the process is hitting calories, steps, workouts and
  habits. Focus on the process (~[56:11]–[57:17]).
- *Score milestones,* for example 90 % or higher every week (~[57:33]–[57:43]).
- *Nightly small wins,* so attention gets trained to notice progress (~[57:44]–[58:55]).
- *High scores need weekly adjustments too.* Perfect habit scores without adjusting intake or activity don't give
  results (~[58:57]–[1:01:06]).
- *"Overload" your habit score:* aim to raise it a little every week (~[1:01:33]–[1:02:28]).
- *Keep momentum:*
  - Replay lessons during dead time (driving, dishes, walking) (~[1:02:42]–[1:03:13]).
  - Spend time with people who share your goals (~[1:03:13]–[1:03:24]).
  - Engage more with the community and your accountability group (~[1:03:24]–[1:04:02]).
- *Rewards that support the goal* (shoes, gym clothes, a fitness watch, a massage, a blender, a course) for
  milestones such as losing 10 lb. Avoid food rewards or cheat days, which can undo the progress
  (~[1:04:02]–[1:05:28]).
- *Book a trip or event ahead of time* as a deadline (~[1:05:33]–[1:06:10]).
- *Baby steps:* set a minimum bar you'll meet no matter what (see the numbers above) (~[1:09:21]–[1:11:46]).
- *No zero days.* Always do something toward the goal. Log your food even after overeating, and journal every
  night (~[1:12:00]–[1:13:36]).
- *Level up your commitment* (~[1:13:44]–[1:18:25]). Mostly already in §5; the new part is telling the group early
  when you feel the dip coming:
  - book a photoshoot;
  - join or create a challenge;
  - post your starting photos or commitment publicly;
  - raise the stakes;
  - tell your group early when you feel the dip coming.
- *Use these tools when it's inconvenient,* not only when you're motivated. That's when they matter
  (~[1:24:21]–[1:26:10]).
- *Welcome the low-motivation stage* as part of the process. If your goals weren't written seriously in week 1,
  rewrite them (~[1:29:29]–[1:30:13]).

**Nutrition**
- *Refeed or diet break:* planned eating at maintenance calories during a cut, not "eat all you can"; it gives
  something to look forward to (~[1:06:11]–[1:06:51]).
- *Hunger rises on a diet* as hunger hormones shift. The transcript calls leptin the hunger hormone and mixes it up
  with ghrelin, so treat the detail as unclear (~[10:18]–[10:26]).
- *Body-fat "set point":* near your personal lowest body fat (e.g. 15 % or 12 %), hunger and lethargy push hard to
  stop dieting (~[1:18:31]–[1:19:11]).
- *Minimum when sick:* eat at maintenance and hit a minimum protein (~[1:10:14]–[1:10:16]).
- *Log food even on bad days* (~[1:12:22]–[1:12:36]).

**New vs already known**
- §5 already has "motivation dips mid-program" and "habits take more than two months". New:
  - the 4-week inspired phase and the 50–66 day window;
  - the concrete minimum-dose numbers (500–1,000 steps, 5 push-ups + 5 squats, 1 light set, 2 days a week);
  - the +1 % a day return ramp;
  - process over outcome, and goal-supporting (non-food) rewards.
- No contradictions. The 66-day figure fits §5's "more than two months".

### 6. My Ultimate Motivation Hack & How to Gain Godlike Focus On The Things That Truly Matter (Week 7 lesson)

**Topic in one line.** Constant quick-reward stimulation (phone, streaming, games, junk food, online arguing) makes
slow-payoff work like training feel boring; lower that stimulation, order the day, and remove distractions so
focus becomes the default.

**Training rules** (almost none)
- Workouts pay off late (no visible change that same evening), which is why they lose to instant-reward activities
  (~[07:38]–[08:23]).
- Caffeine and pre-workout lose their effect with regular use (tolerance). He uses this only as an analogy
  (~[09:45]–[10:01]).
- *"Productive procrastination":* skipping the gym to do something that only looks productive (watching tutorials,
  studying crypto, fixing a website) is still procrastination (~[1:30:56]–[1:31:42]).
- The coach trains 5 times a week as part of a deliberately simple life (~[44:44]–[44:47]). That's his routine, not
  a prescription.

**Cardio, steps, sleep and recovery**
- Walk (steps) while reviewing your vision, as a replacement for the morning phone check (~[43:31]–[43:41]).
- Charge the phone far from where you are and use a separate alarm clock, so the phone is out of sight
  (~[1:32:55]–[1:33:22]). Useful for sleep too.
- Do Not Disturb, airplane or silent mode during focus time (~[1:34:54]–[1:35:10]).
- Silence and meditation lower stimulation, e.g. driving with no audio (~[1:28:56]–[1:29:50], [1:14:03]–[1:14:18]).

**Habits and mindset that could become small daily quests**
- *No phone for the first part of the morning.* He calls it the single biggest lever and suggests trying it for one
  week (~[40:46]–[43:06], [1:18:29]–[1:18:42]). Replace the habit rather than just deleting it: meditate, read a few
  pages, play an audiobook, or walk while reviewing your vision (~[43:10]–[43:45]).
- *Order the day.* Do the important, low-stimulation tasks first. Save high-stimulation treats (streaming, social
  media) for the end of the day, only after the important tasks are done and only if they're not harmful
  (~[38:20]–[40:38], [1:17:27]–[1:18:07]). Good fit for an evening "reward unlocked" mechanic.
- *Don't stack stimulation* (streaming plus snacks plus phone at once) to escape boredom. It kills boredom for a few
  hours but makes tomorrow's tasks feel duller (~[28:00]–[28:54], [1:15:28]–[1:15:36]).
- *Self-check for overstimulation:* can't put the phone down, poor focus, can't stand silence, compulsive habits,
  chasing shiny objects (~[15:43]–[15:55]).
- *Boredom reframe:* feeling bored is your brain getting a rest (~[1:13:15]–[1:14:18]). This is a claim; see the
  risks section.
- *Three detox methods* (~[1:14:29]–[1:19:07]):
  1. *Cold turkey:* stop a trigger completely. The coach's preference.
  2. *A fast:* avoid a trigger for 1–4 weeks, then set strict limits. Example: 2 weeks off social media, then check
     it once a day.
  3. *"Progressive underload":* cut exposure step by step. Example: screens 2 h, then 1 h, then 30 min, then 0;
     desserts 3 days a week, then 2, then 1, then none.

  These fit a time-limited, opt-in challenge format well.
- *Define your few important areas yourself* (for him: mission, family, relationship, health and fitness, personal
  development, "productive fun") and say no to what doesn't support them (~[48:50]–[49:27], [52:46]–[54:36]).
  Trade-offs are unavoidable because time is limited (~[55:43]–[56:06]).
- *Subtract, don't add.* Ask what you can remove, outsource or systematise (~[56:19]–[56:53]). Focus is what's left
  when distractions are gone (~[51:44]–[52:18]).
- *Singular focus beats scattered focus.* Spread over 8 things, each gets about 12.5 % (~[59:30]–[1:00:10]).
  Beginners should do one thing at a time (~[1:27:13]–[1:27:33]).
- *Multitasking is really task switching;* do one thing at a time (~[1:06:34]–[1:08:21]).
- *Schedule "productive fun" now and then:* trips, friends, hobbies, breaks (~[45:31]–[45:46], [1:10:14]–[1:10:27]).
- *Curate your inputs.* Unfollow or mute unhelpful accounts. Take some distance from people who keep pulling you
  off your goals; you can still care about them from afar (~[1:19:28]–[1:20:38]).
- *Focus tools* (~[1:30:04]–[1:35:10]):
  - feed blockers for social sites;
  - website and app blockers with a hard lock (e.g. 1 hour that can't be cancelled);
  - a focus timer that grows a virtual tree, 25 min up to 2 h, where leaving the app kills the tree. The transcript
    says "4S", probably Forest. It's a gamified focus quest close to LevelMeUp's focus challenges;
  - deleting high-stimulation apps from the phone.
- *Interruptions are costly:* after one, getting back into deep focus takes about 15–20 minutes. This is a claim
  (~[1:35:14]–[1:35:34]).
- *Pick a few tools and stick with them;* don't keep adding productivity apps (~[1:36:08]–[1:36:46]).
- *Cut daily decisions:* same meals on "default" days, same outfit (~[45:02]–[45:07]).

**Nutrition**
- Highly palatable food (sweet, salty, flavour-enhanced) raises the bar, so plain healthy food tastes dull. Cutting
  back makes simple food enjoyable again (~[22:34]–[24:30]).
- Repeat the same meals on default days (~[45:02]–[45:05]).
- Use progressive underload for desserts and junk food (~[1:17:12]–[1:17:18]).

**New vs already known**
- §5 only says to cut back on cheap dopamine and use a countdown widget. New: the three detox methods with numbers,
  the "important first, treats last" day order, the morning phone rule ranked first, replacement habits, the
  focus-tool list, and "subtract, don't add".
- No countdown widget is mentioned in this video, so §5's widget point probably comes from the Week 7 action-item
  PDF.
- Unclear transcript:
  - The file is numbered "6." but the speaker calls it Week 7 (~[00:00]).
  - Around [1:37:22]–[1:38:00] the transcript is just "Bye!" repeated (a Whisper artefact), so a short closing
    passage may be missing.

### Week 10 - The Invisible Force Preventing You From Reaching Your Full Potential

**Topic in one line.** Limiting beliefs, including ones that are technically true, quietly block consistency and
adjustment. Beginners should ask "is it true?" and informed people should ask "does it serve me long term?". Bonus:
eat more protein.

**Training rules**
- You don't need to train every day. Beginners often think they must train 5–7 times a week; the real keys are rest
  and progressive overload (~[09:56]–[10:02], [14:31]–[14:37]).
- Progressive overload is called the key to building muscle, with the aim of progressing every workout
  (~[30:08]–[30:14]).
- Strength loss during a long cut is normal, especially near low body fat; still try to progress
  (~[16:57]–[17:05], [30:46]–[30:58]).
- Getting smaller on a cut doesn't mean losing muscle. Arms, chest, glutes and legs all carry fat, so they shrink.
  Use strength progress, not tape measurements, as the muscle signal (~[59:04]–[59:58]).
- Fear that adding weight causes injury blocks progressive overload (~[58:38]–[59:01]). Fear of squats and deadlifts
  often comes from injury clips online; the coaches remove those posts from the group (~[1:05:41]–[1:06:06]).
- People with the best results usually have fixed elements in their week: a set workout schedule and repeated
  meals (~[38:05]–[38:42]).
- The coaching style aims slightly toward pressure on a comfort-versus-pressure scale (~[40:34]–[41:48]).

**Cardio, steps, sleep and recovery**
- "15k steps is too much" is listed as a belief that blocks adjustment. If weight stalls even at about 10–15k steps,
  something still has to change (~[58:12]–[58:32]).
- Nothing on sleep or recovery beyond the rest-day point above.

**Habits and mindset that could become small daily quests**
- *Three stages* (~[09:36]–[34:28]):
  1. Lack of knowledge. The fix is to learn.
  2. Knowledge but unhelpful beliefs. The fix is to revise or "unlearn" them.
  3. Knowledge plus helpful beliefs, which is where the big results come.
- *Two questions:* a beginner asks whether a belief is true (~[12:01]–[12:13]). Someone already informed asks
  whether it serves them, ideally over the long term (~[20:44]–[21:32], [50:03]–[50:16]).
- *Rationalisations travel with limiting beliefs* (~[17:20]–[17:37], [24:13]–[24:36], [48:13]–[48:38]). Examples:
  - "I wear a shirt anyway."
  - "Fitness isn't a race."
  - "Slow progress beats none."
- *Beliefs that block consistency* (~[52:23]–[52:44]), each to be questioned with "is it true?" and "does it serve
  me?" (~[52:59]–[53:31]):
  - repeating meals is dumb, variety is better;
  - I can't enjoy gatherings without food;
  - I feel deprived without my favourite foods;
  - my genetics are bad;
  - fitness isn't that important;
  - I have plenty of time;
  - failing is fine;
  - I don't need to push myself.
- *Childhood food rules* (for example, always finishing your plate) can drive overeating as an adult
  (~[53:38]–[55:07]).
- *What worked before may not work for the next level* (~[32:54]–[34:16]).
- *Six steps to work on a limiting belief* (~[1:06:40]–[1:10:44]). This is a natural journal quest:
  1. Identify it. Ask why you're not getting results even though you know what to do.
  2. Accept that it's a belief, not a fact, so it can change.
  3. Challenge it: is it true, and does it serve you?
  4. Picture carrying it for 5–20 years, to build leverage. He credits this to Tony Robbins.
  5. Choose a new belief that serves the goal.
  6. Act as that person repeatedly: how would they think and decide?
- *Get an outside view* from a coach or someone you trust, because your own beliefs are hard to spot
  (~[1:08:40]–[1:08:54]).
- *Failure and pressure.* The coach keeps a high standard ("not OK to fail") but works on bouncing back quickly
  without beating himself up. He admits pressure doesn't suit everyone, and says to judge any stance by its
  long-term effect (~[42:39]–[51:21]). Delay has an opportunity cost (~[50:47]–[50:56]).
- *Weekly review* reminder, done that same night (~[1:13:33]–[1:13:36]).

**Nutrition**
- Protein has the highest thermic effect of food. The transcript says roughly 20–30 % for protein versus 3–5 % for
  the others, though the figures are partly garbled. Hitting protein daily therefore helps weight loss, and protein
  also keeps you fuller (~[01:04]–[03:43]). The example eater goes above 1 g per lb (~[02:18]–[02:20]).
- Fewer food choices means less decision fatigue and more consistency. Deciding what to eat every 3 hours and
  hunting for entries in a food app is tiring (~[23:23]–[23:40], [30:27]–[30:40]).
- When a cut stalls, adjust intake or activity rather than refusing to go below a set number. The number is garbled
  ("1-2 calories"), probably 1,200 kcal (~[57:21]–[58:26]). See the risks section.
- A pinch test of side-belly fat is said to outrank BMI (~[1:03:48]–[1:05:02]). See the risks section.

**New vs already known**
- New compared with §5: the thermic-effect reason for protein, "you don't need daily training", "smaller isn't
  muscle loss, use strength as the signal", the belief framework, and the six-step journal method.
- Tension and contradictions:
  - The Journal Guide in §5 says to restart after a slip without self-blame, while this video promotes "not OK to
    fail". His coping advice does match "don't beat yourself up".
  - The video frames wanting a diet break or refeed during a stall as a possible excuse (~[1:00:47]–[1:00:51]),
    while §5 treats planned diet breaks as good practice.
  - "Progress every workout" is faster than §6.4's suggested one step-up per week. That's an app suggestion, not a
    §5 rule.

### Anything risky for a beginner app

- **Shaming and exclusion** (Week 1, ~[1:46:17]–[1:47:05]). A weekly score below 75 % turns red so coaches can
  call the person out, and three red weeks remove them from the leaderboard and their group, described as removing
  bad apples. This conflicts with LevelMeUp's no-shaming rule.
- **Public scores as pressure** (Week 1, ~[1:44:48]–[1:46:12]; Week 5). Leaning on fear of disappointing coaches
  or "parents" (Week 1, ~[1:10:40]–[1:10:49]) and on public scores. In the app this must be opt-in only.
- **Money penalties** (Week 5, ~[1:15:57]–[1:17:40], [1:30:43]).
  - Group fines raised from 100 to 1,000 (currency not stated, presumably pesos) for each missed calorie or step
    target, so that missing hurts.
  - Spending on the most expensive programs to force commitment (~[1:21:31]–[1:23:35]).

  Both are financial harm and pressure; never put them in the app.
- **Training and step quotas while ill** (Week 5, ~[1:10:05]–[1:10:28], [1:13:04]–[1:13:21]). Light sets 3 times a
  week and minimum steps during a COVID infection, plus "no zero days". LevelMeUp must offer real rest without
  penalty when someone is sick.
- **Chasing very low body fat** (Week 5, ~[1:18:31]–[1:21:11]; Week 10, ~[16:45]–[18:37], [59:58]–[1:01:30];
  Week 1, ~[1:47:31]). This includes pushing past your body-fat "set point" (e.g. 15 % to 10 %) by borrowing
  outside pressure, and putting up with hunger for weeks or months. 10 % body fat is treated as the default male
  goal.
- **Refusing a calorie floor** (Week 10, ~[57:21]–[58:26]). The video treats refusing to go below about 1,200 kcal
  (number garbled) as a limiting belief, which implies cutting lower when weight stalls. Too aggressive without a
  coach.
- **Ignoring BMI and family** (Week 10, ~[1:01:37]–[1:05:02]).
  - People who say you've become too thin are framed as obstacles to ignore.
  - Someone at 40–50 kg flagged as underweight by BMI is told to keep cutting if they can still pinch side-belly
    fat.

  This carries a disordered-eating risk; it must never appear in the app.
- **Diet breaks framed as excuses** (Week 10, ~[1:00:47]–[1:00:51]). Treating a wish for a refeed or diet break
  during a stall as a rationalisation could push beginners to skip recovery tools that §5 endorses.
- **Rigid dieting for fast results** (Week 10, ~[36:41]–[38:42]). Adopting "bro bodybuilder" rigidity (very
  restricted, repetitive food) is presented as the route to fast, dramatic results. Risky as a default for
  beginners.
- **"Not OK to fail"** (Week 10, ~[42:39]–[51:21]). This, plus the advice that a new belief needn't be true as
  long as it helps (~[1:10:17]–[1:10:24]), can feed perfectionism or adopting false beliefs. Keep the bounce-back part and drop the
  pressure part.
- **Hypnosis** (Week 5, ~[51:04]–[55:44]). A guided group "self-hypnosis" session run by an outside facilitator is
  presented as subconscious reprogramming. Not for the app.
